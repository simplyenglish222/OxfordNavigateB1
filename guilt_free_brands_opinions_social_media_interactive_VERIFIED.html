Subtopic 1

Guilt-free brands and everyday shopping

Buying things is a normal part of life. We need food, clothes, phones and other products. But sometimes a consumer starts to think about what happened before a product reached the shop. Was a worker paid fairly? Were the working conditions safe? Did the factory create a lot of pollution? These questions can make people feel guilty about shopping.

A person may feel guilty for several reasons. A very cheap T-shirt may be attractive because it saves money, but it may have been produced by poorly paid workers. A new phone may be exciting, but producing electronics can cause damage to the environment. Even food can make people feel bad if it comes in too much plastic or travels thousands of kilometres. The problem is that ordinary shoppers often do not know the full story behind a brand.

This is why some companies sell an ethical product or describe themselves as a guilt-free brand. The idea is simple: a company tries to make a product in a way that is better for the planet and better for workers. Some companies use fair trade materials. Others try to reduce waste, use recycled packaging, or make products that last longer. A phone that is easy to repair may be used for five years instead of two. That can reduce the need to throw away electronics.

However, ethical shopping is not always simple. A more responsible product may have a higher price. Some people cannot afford it. A student may want to buy an ethical jacket but choose a cheaper one because the expensive one costs half of a monthly budget. This does not automatically mean that the student does not care about the planet. Money is a real part of every shopping decision.

There is also another question: should we buy a “better” product, or should we simply buy less? A company can raise awareness about a problem, but it still wants people to buy its products. Some critics say that a green or ethical label can give people permission to buy more and more stuff. Instead of replacing a phone every two years, they say we should keep it until it really stops working.

There are practical things people can do. We can buy local when it makes sense, choose second-hand clothes, recycle what we can, and learn more about a company’s supply chain. We can also ask whether we really need a new item. There is rarely one perfect responsible choice. The useful habit is to stop for a moment, get some information, and make a choice that fits both our values and our real-life budget.

Another useful habit is to compare the whole life of a product, not only the label on the box. A cheap item that breaks after six months may create more waste than a more expensive item that can last longer. Before buying, a consumer can ask: Do I need it now? Can I buy it second-hand? Can I repair the old one? Small questions like these make ethical shopping more practical and less about perfect labels.

Subtopic 2

Giving and asking for opinions

People discuss small decisions every day. Friends decide where to eat. A family chooses a holiday. Students decide how to divide a group project. Colleagues talk about the best way to solve a problem. In all these situations, we need to ask for an opinion, give an opinion, agree or disagree without making the conversation unpleasant.

The simplest question is “What do you think about ...?” It is useful because it is open and neutral. Another common question is “Don’t you think ...?” This is a little different because the speaker already has an idea and expects the other person to react to it. For example: “Don’t you think this phone is too expensive?” The second question can sound stronger, so your voice and situation matter.

When you want to give an opinion, you have many choices. “In my opinion, ...” is clear and common. “If you ask me, ...” is more conversational. “As far as I’m concerned, ...” is useful when you want to make it clear that this is your personal point of view. “I’m convinced that ...” is stronger. Use it when you feel very sure.

Good discussions are not only about saying your own idea. You also need to react. “That’s a good point.” shows that you understand an argument. “I see what you mean.” shows understanding even if you do not completely agree. “You may be right, but ...” is a useful way to disagree politely. “I’m not sure about that.” is softer than simply saying “You are wrong.”

It also helps to give a reason. Compare these two answers: “Electric cars are better.” and “I think electric cars are better because they can reduce pollution in cities.” The second answer is easier to discuss because the other person knows your reason. You can also use an example from real life to support your opinion. This makes your speaking longer and clearer.

Sometimes we use information from another person or organisation. Then “according to” is useful: “According to the manufacturer, this battery should last five years.” Notice that “according to me” is normally not used for a personal opinion. Say “In my opinion” instead.

A successful discussion does not require two people to agree. The goal is to exchange ideas, understand the other point of view, and perhaps change your mind. You can be confident without being aggressive. Ask a clear question, listen to the answer, explain your reason, and react to what the other person actually said. That is much more useful than waiting silently for your turn to speak.

For speaking practice, short answers are a good beginning, but they should grow. Try a simple three-step pattern: state your opinion, give a reason, and then add an example. After that, ask the other person a question. For instance: “If you ask me, repairing the old phone is better because it saves money. I repaired mine last year and it still works. What do you think about that?” This pattern is simple, but it makes a real conversation continue naturally.

Subtopic 3

Writing for social media

Social media lets people publish ideas very quickly. A user can write a post, add a photo, use a hashtag, and send the idea to hundreds of people in a few seconds. Other users can like, share or write a comment. This makes online communication fast and useful, but it also means that people need to write clearly and think before they publish.

A good social-media post normally has one main idea. A short headline can tell the audience what the post is about. After that, the writer can explain the situation and give a reason for an opinion. For example, a person may ask whether cheap clothes are ethical. They can explain that some clothes are produced in poor working conditions, but they can also mention that many people cannot afford more expensive clothes.

When you write a comment, it is useful to react to the original idea instead of changing the subject completely. You can say, “I see what you mean, but…” or “That’s a good point, because…” A respectful comment can disagree strongly without attacking another person. Online conversations become much worse when people insult each other instead of discussing the idea.

You should also check the facts before you share a serious claim. Imagine that you see a post saying that a famous company uses child labour. The information may be true, but it may also be old or misleading. Before sharing it, try to find out where the information came from. Look for a reliable source, not only a dramatic picture or an angry comment. A post can go viral before anyone checks whether it is correct.

Social media also influences shopping. Companies advertise products in your feed, and influencers can influence what their followers want to buy. An online review may help you choose a phone, restaurant or hotel, but some reviews are fake. It is useful to compare several sources before spending money.

Fashion is a good example. Fast fashion helps people keep up with fashion at a low price. Clothes can go out of fashion quickly, so some people buy new things often. Others argue that we should buy fewer clothes because cheap production can harm workers and the environment. There is also a real economic question: if customers stop buying from factories in poorer countries, could this put people out of work?

When writing online, remember that a real person may read your words. Protect your privacy, think before you upload personal information, and respect other people. You can always delete a post later, but screenshots may remain. A useful rule is simple: be clear, give a reason, check important facts, and write something you would be comfortable saying face to face.

It is also useful to think about tone. A message can sound much stronger online because the reader cannot hear your voice or see your face. Before you reply to a comment, read your words once more. If the message sounds angry, make it more respectful. You can disagree with the idea without attacking the person. This is especially important when the topic is money, politics, work, or personal choices, where people often have very different experiences.

I see what you mean when people say cheap clothes are useful because not everyone can afford expensive brands. However, if you ask me, we should think more about how clothes are made. Very low prices can sometimes mean that workers are poorly paid or work in bad conditions. At the same time, simply refusing to buy products from poorer countries may put some people out of work. I think a practical solution is to buy fewer clothes, keep them for longer, and check which companies treat workers properly. I also buy second-hand clothes sometimes. They are cheaper, and buying them creates less waste. So I don’t think every cheap item is unethical, but consumers should try to make a responsible choice.
